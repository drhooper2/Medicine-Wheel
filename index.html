Indigenous Perspective in the B.C. Curriculum

Incorporating Indigenous teachings into classrooms in British Columbia is essential for fostering respect, understanding, and reconciliation. These teachings offer rich cultural knowledge, diverse worldviews, and holistic approaches to learning that benefit all students. Tools like the Medicine Wheel introduce learners to the interconnectedness of the physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual aspects of life, encouraging balance and self-awareness. Including Indigenous perspectives not only aligns with B.C.’s curriculum goals of honoring First Peoples Principles of Learning but also helps create inclusive spaces where Indigenous students feel seen and valued. Ultimately, these teachings enrich educational experiences and help build more respectful and informed communities.


The Medicine Wheel is a powerful symbol and teaching tool used by many Indigenous cultures across North America, though its specific meanings and interpretations can vary by Nation and region. Despite these differences, the core concepts of the Medicine Wheel—such as balance, interconnectedness, and the importance of the mental, physical, emotional, and spiritual aspects of life—offer valuable lessons for everyone. By learning from the teachings of the Medicine Wheel, we can deepen our understanding of Indigenous worldviews, foster greater respect for the land and each other, and promote holistic well-being in ourselves and our communities. Embracing these teachings, with humility and respect, supports a more inclusive and meaningful approach to education and life.

They help us understand different ways of thinking and remind us to live in a balanced and respectful way.

First Peoples Principles of Learning

The First Peoples Principles of Learning (FPPL) were developed by Indigenous educators and knowledge keepers in British Columbia. They reflect Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and teaching, and they guide educators in integrating Indigenous perspectives into learning environments. These principles emphasize the holistic, experiential, and relational aspects of learning.


  • Learning ultimately supports the well-being of the community, the land,
    the spirits, and the ancestors.
  • Learning is holistic, reflective, experiential, and relational
    (focused on connectedness, or reciprocal relationships, and a sense
    of place).
  • Learning involves recognizing the consequences of one’s actions.
  • Learning involves generational roles and responsibilities.
  • Learning recognized the role of Indigenous Knowledge.
  • Learning is embedded in memory, history, and story.
  • Learning involves patience and time.
  • Learning requires exploration of one’s identity.
  • Learning involves recognizing that some knowledge is
    sacred and only shared with permission and/or in certain situations.


Acknowledging Diverse Teachings of the Medicine Wheel

When introducing the Medicine Wheel to students, it is important for educators to recognize that there is no single, universal way to teach or understand this sacred symbol. Teachings about the Medicine Wheel vary across Indigenous Nations, each reflecting the unique worldview, language, and traditions of that community. While the colors, directions, and associated meanings may differ, the Medicine Wheel commonly represents interconnectedness, balance, and holistic well-being. By acknowledging the diversity of teachings, educators honor the rich cultural knowledge of Indigenous Peoples and foster a respectful and inclusive learning environment. When possible, connecting with local Elders or Knowledge Keepers can help ensure that teachings are shared in a culturally appropriate and meaningful way.


It is always important to honour and respect cultural protocol.

Lesson Plans

Introduction to the colours of the Medicine Wheel

Grade Level: K
Duration: 60-75 minutes
Subjects: Art Education, Social Studies
Location: Classroom & Outdoors

Big Ideas (BC Curriculum):

  • Stories and traditions help us learn about ourselves, our families, and our communities.
  • People connect to others and share who they are through stories and traditions.

First Peoples Principles of Learning:

  • Learning is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential, and relational.
  • Learning involves recognizing the consequences of one’s actions.
  • Learning involves generational roles and responsibilities.

Learning Objectives:

Students will:

  • Learn the medicine wheel has four quadrants (sections) and each quadrant has its own colour (white, yellow, red and black).
  • Recognize the shape of the Medicine Wheel is a circle.
  • Practice respect and curiosity when learning about Indigenous knowledge.

Materials:

  • Large poster or digital display of the Medicine Wheel
  • Blank Medicine Wheel Template
  • Crayons/markers in white, yellow, red, and black
  • Picture books or short video introducing the Medicine Wheel (eg., All Creation Represented: A Child's Guide to the Medicine Wheel - by Joyce Perreault)
  • Small talking stick (or other object) or sharing item (optional for circle time)

Lesson Activities:

1: Welcome Circle (5–10 min)

  • Sit in a circle.
  • Begin with a land acknowledgment.
  • Talk about the importance of sitting in a circle;
    • everyone is equally important in a circle
    • there is no beginning or end to a circle
    • there is balance in a circle
    • we are stronger in a circle when we support each other
  • Inform students that they will be learning about a special circle called a Medicine Wheel.

2: Introduction to the Medicine Wheel (10 min)

  • Show a image of a Medicine Wheel and ask students what they see (a circle, there are four parts).
  • Share with students that the Medicine Wheel is an Indigenous symbol of strength, balance and well-being.
  • Ask students how many parts they see inside the circle.
  • Talk about the four "equal" quadrants (sections). Explain that "each quadrant has its own colour:" [This will be important as they learn more about additional aspects of the Medicine Wheel]
    • White (top)
    • Yellow (right)
    • Red (bottom)
    • Black (left)
  • Remind students that each part of the Medicine Wheel is equally important and interconnected.


3: Story or Video (15-30 margin-right)

  • Read a story or show a short video about the Medicine Wheel or Indigenous teachings. (check resource list at the end of this lesson)

4: Hands-On Activity (10–15 min)
  • Give students a blank Medicine Wheel worksheet.
  • Guide students to color each section with the correct color, and print the colour words inside the appropriate box.
    • Print and colour wheel
  • Optional: Ask students to draw something in each section that makes them think of the colours (white, yellow, red and black).

5: Sharing Circle (5–10 min)
  • Invite students to share one part of their wheel.
  • Use a talking stick (or other object) to encourage respectful listening and speaking.

Assessment & Extension

Assessment:

  • Observe students interaction and participation.
  • Check for understanding through completed Medicine Wheel worksheets (did they colour each section with the corresponding colour, did they print the colour words in the appropriate box).

Extensions:

  • Add songs or drumming activities.
  • Make a class Medicine Wheel that includes all ideas.

Exploring Directions on the Medicine Wheel

Grade Level: 1
Duration: 75-90 minutes
Subjects: Art Education, Social Studies
Location: Classroom & Outdoors

Big Ideas (BC Curriculum):

  • Indigenous knowledge is passed down through oral history, traditions, and collective memory.
  • Learning about ourselves and others helps us build respectful relationships.
  • Stories and traditions connect us to the past and help us understand our place in the world.

First Peoples Principles of Learning:

  • Learning is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential, and relational.
  • Learning involves recognizing the consequences of one’s actions.
  • Learning recognizes the role of Indigenous knowledge systems and worldviews.

Learning Objectives:

Students will:

  • Identify and explain the four quadrants of the Medicine Wheel.
  • Show respect and curiosity when learning from Indigenous teachings.

Materials:

  • Poster or projected image of the Medicine Wheel
  • Blank Medicine Wheel Template
  • Crayons/markers in white, yellow, red, and black
  • Journals or reflection sheets
  • Picture books or short video introducing the Medicine Wheel (e.g., All Creation Represented: A Child's Guide to the Medicine Wheel - by Joyce Perreault)).
  • Small talking stick (or other object) or sharing item (optional for circle time)

Lesson Activities:

1: Opening Discussion (15 min)

Sit in a circle:

  • Begin with a land acknowledgment.
  • Ask students: "What do you know about the Medicine Wheel?" (colours, shape)
  • Tell students they will be learning about the four directions on the Medicine Wheel today.
  • Talk about the four directions of a map, and using a map compass, have students locate north, east, south, and west. Explain to students that the Medicine Wheel identifies directions, just as a map identifies directions.
  • Depending on students understanding, or what they remember, you may need to remind them that the Medicine Wheel is used by many Indigenous communities as a guide for balance and well-being (and there are many interpretations across the nations).

2: Teach the Four Quadrants (10–15 min)

  • Discuss each quadrant (section) of the wheel:
    • North (white)
    • East (yellow)
    • South (red)
    • West (black)
  • Connect each quadrant on the wheel template to a direction,
    (e.g., north = white, east = yellow, south = red, west = black).


3: Story Connection (10–15 min)

  • Read an Indigenous story or watch a short video to learn more about the Medicine Wheel.
  • Prompt students to think about the quadrants of the Medicine Wheel and see if they can make connections to their own life.

4: Creative Activity (20-30 min)
  • Place signs for North, East, South, and West on walls around the classroom or outside.
  • Students start in the center of the room. You call out a direction, and they walk (or crawl, or flap like eagles!) to the correct wall.
  • Add storytelling prompts:
    • “Fly to the East where the sun rises!”
    • “Bear walks to the North to find peace.”
  • After movement activity, have students complete a Medicine Wheel template worksheet: label and colour each quadrant by printing the direction and colouring each section in appropriatly. Blank Medicine Wheel Template

5: Reflection and Sharing Circle (20min)
  • Invite students to share their reflections with the class.

Assessment & Extension

Assessment:

  • Participation in discussion and sharing
  • Completed Medicine Wheel worksheet
  • Journal or reflection entries showing understanding of concepts covered

Extensions:

  • Integrate art by making a large collaborative classroom Medicine Wheel.

Deepening Understanding through the Seasons

Grade Level: 2
Duration: 60-90 minutes
Subjects: Art Education, Social Studies
Location: Classroom & Outdoors

Big Ideas (BC Curriculum):

  • Indigenous peoples have rich cultural traditions and perspectives passed down through oral history, traditions, and collective memory.
  • Exploring different worldviews helps us develop empathy, respect, and understanding of others.
  • Well-being includes physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual aspects.

First Peoples Principles of Learning:

  • Learning is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential, and relational.
  • Learning involves generational roles and responsibilities.
  • Learning involves recognizing the consequences of one’s actions.
  • Learning recognizes Indigenous knowledge systems and worldviews.

Learning Objectives:

Students will:

  • Explain the symbolism and teachings of the Medicine Wheel.
  • Connect each quadrant of the wheel to personal experiences and community well-being.
  • Understand the cyclical nature of time and seasons in Indigenous worldviews.
  • Engage in respectful discussion of Indigenous knowledge.

Materials:

  • Visual of the Medicine Wheel with labeled directions, colors,(you will be adding the seasons with this lesson)
  • Blank Medicine Wheel Template
  • Chart paper or whiteboard
  • Markers or colored pencils
  • Picture books or short video on the Medicine Wheel
    (e.g., The Medicine Wheel: Stories of a Hoop Dancer - by Teddy Anderson;
    Shin chi's Canoe     - by Nicole Campbell).
  • Reflection journal or writing paper
  • Small talking stick (or other object) or sharing item (optional for circle time)

Lesson Activities:

1: Introduction & Land Acknowledgment (5–10 min)

Sit in a circle:

  • Begin with a land acknowledgment.
  • Ask students: "What do you know about Indigenous teachings or ways of understanding the world?"
    "What do you know about the Medicine Wheel?" (colours and directions)
  • Depending on students understanding, or what they remember, you may need to remind them that the Medicine Wheel is used by many Indigenous communities as a guide for balance and well-being (and there are many interpretations across the nations).

2: Teaching the Medicine Wheel (15–20 min)

  • Show a detailed image of the Medicine Wheel with four quadrants - today you will add the seasons.
  • Connect each season to a quadrant (e.g., spring is yellow, the sun is yellow and it comes up in the east; summer is red, and there are beautiful sunsets and warm weather in the south; in the fall the clocks turn back an hour and it gets dark (black) earlier; winter in the north, lots of white snow).
    • Winter (north)
    • Spring (east)
    • Summer (south)
    • Fall (west)
  • Ask students what they, and their families, do during the different seasons; what do they think
    Indigenous people used to do long ago during each season. Talk about the differences and similarities.
  • Keep in mind that different Nations may interpret the wheel differently.


3: Story Connection (15–20 min)

  • Read or view an Indigenous story that reflects balance, growth, or seasonal cycles.
  • Prompt discussion: "Which parts of the Medicine Wheel can we see in this story?"

4: Student Activity (20–30 min)
  • Brainstorm as a class, what they do during the different seasons, and how these activities contribute to a healthy lifestyle.
  • Students complete a Medicine Wheel template: label each quadrant and write or draw an example of something they do during the different seasons to maintain a healthy lifestyle.

5: Reflection and Sharing Circle (20min)
  • Invite students to share insights or something new they learned.
  • Encourage respectful listening and acknowledgment of different perspectives.

Assessment & Extension

Assessment:

  • Participation in discussions, reflection and sharing
  • Completed Medicine Wheel worksheet with personal connections
  • Journal or reflection entries showing understanding of concepts covered

Extensions:

  • Create a classroom mural or collaborative wheel with shared ideas.
  • Integrate science or health topics (e.g., body systems, emotional regulation) through the lens of the Medicine Wheel.

Animals of the Medicine Wheel

Grade Level: 3
Duration: 45-60 minutes
Subjects: Art Education, Social Studies
Location: Classroom & Outdoors

Big Ideas (BC Curriculum):

  • Stories and traditions help us understand ourselves and connect with others.
  • Indigenous knowledge is passed through oral history, traditions, and collective memory.

First Peoples Principles of Learning:

  • Learning is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential, and relational.
  • Learning involves generational roles and responsibilities.
  • Learning involves patience and time..

Learning Objectives:

Students will:

  • Learn about four animals of the Medicine Wheel (Grizzly Bear, Eagle, Buffalo, and Wolf).
  • Understand the symbolic meanings of these animals and how they relate to different directions.
  • Reflect on how these animals can teach us important life lessons.
  • Practice respectful learning of Indigenous knowledge through stories, drawing, and discussion.

Materials:

  • Large visual of the Medicine Wheel (with animals in each quadrant)
  • Printable student journals or drawing pages (e.g., Medicine Wheel templates)
  • Blank Medicine Wheel Template
  • Markers or colored pencils
  • Picture books or short video on the Medicine Wheel
  • Reflection journal or writing paper
  • Small talking stick (or other object) or sharing item (optional for circle time)

Lesson Activities:

1: Welcome Circle (5–10 min)

Sit in a circle:

  • Begin with a land acknowledgment.
  • Ask students: "What do you know about Indigenous teachings or ways of understanding the world?"
    "What do you know about the Medicine Wheel?"
  • Depending on students understanding, or what they remember, you may need to remind them that the Medicine Wheel is used by many Indigenous communities as a guide for balance and well-being (and there are many interpretations across the nations).
  • Ask: "What do we know about animals that help people?"

2: Introduction to the Medicine Wheel Animals (10–15 min)

  • Present the Medicine Wheel with the four animals:
    • Grizzly Bear (north) – strength, courage, introspection
    • Eagle (east) – vision, spirituality, guidance
    • Wolf (south) – learning, relationships, guardianship
    • Buffalo (west) – respect, sharing, gratitude
  • Show images or short video clips of each animal.
  • Keep in mind that different Nations may interpret the wheel differently.
  • Discuss how each animal represents important values.


3: Storytime or Video (10–15 min)

  • Read a picture book or show a video about one or more of the animals and their traditional teachings.
  • Suggested Book: "The Song Within My Heart" by David Bouchard — a story that includes teachings about the drum and the connection to nature and animals.
  • Suggested Video: A short clip from “Four Sacred Medicines” by Elder Mary Wilson or a video from your local Indigenous Education team, if available.
  • Example: A story of the Eagle and its ability to see far or the Bear's strength during hibernation.

4: Hands-On Journal Activity (10–15 min)
  • Provide each student with a Medicine Wheel template.
  • Guide students to draw the four animals in their respective directions.
  • Invite them to write or draw what each animal teaches us.

5: Sharing Circle (5–10 min)
  • Invite students to share which animal they feel connected to and why. (This can also be connected to the Seven Grandfather Teachings>
  • Use a talking stick or object to ensure respectful listening,encouraging respectful listening and acknowledgment of different perspectives.

Assessment & Extension

Assessment:

  • Observe student participation and engagement in circle discussions.
  • Review completed Medicine Wheel drawings and journal responses for understanding.

Extensions:

  • Create a class “Animal Wheel” with everyone's chosen animal.
  • Learn a song or drumming rhythm associated with one of the animals. (Ask the Indigenous Education Worker)
  • Invite an Indigenous Knowledge Keeper or Elder to speak about animal teachings.

Elements of the Medicine Wheel

Grade Level: 4
Duration: 60 minutes
Subjects: Art Education, Social Studies
Location: Classroom & Outdoors

Big Ideas (BC Curriculum):

  • People’s beliefs and values are shaped by their experiences and cultures
  • Interactions between people and the environment shape our world.
  • Indigenous knowledge is passed down through oral history, stories, and traditions.

First Peoples Principles of Learning:

  • Learning is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential, and relational.
  • Learning is embedded in memory, history, and story.
  • Learning involves recognizing the consequences of one’s actions.
  • Learning requires exploration of one’s identity.

Learning Objectives:

Students will:

  • Identify the four elements represented in the Medicine Wheel (Air, Earth, Fire, Water).
  • Understand the symbolism and teachings connected to each element.
  • Recognize the traditional directional and color associations with each element.
  • Practice respectful learning of Indigenous knowledge through stories, drawing, and discussion.
  • Reflect on their own connection to nature and the elements.

Materials:

  • Large Medicine Wheel visual (with labeled elements)
  • Printable Medicine Wheel templates (students will add the elements)
  • Blank Medicine Wheel Template
  • Colored pencils/crayons (white, yellow, red, black)
  • Storybook or video introducing the elements and the Medicine Wheel
  • Access to nature (if possible, short outdoor walk or observation)
  • Chart paper and markers for group discussion
  • Reflection journal or writing paper
  • Small talking stick (or other object) or sharing item (optional for circle time)

Lesson Activities:

1: Introduction & Circle Discussion (10–15 min)

Sit in a circle:

  • Begin with a land acknowledgment.
  • Ask students: “What do you know about the four elements?"
  • Introduce the Medicine Wheel as a way Indigenous Peoples connect to the natural world and find balance.
  • Briefly explain the elements:
    • Air (north): breath, communication, mind
    • Earth (east): growth, grounding, body
    • Fire (south): passion, energy, spirit
    • Water (west): emotion, intuition, heartlife


2: Story or Video (10–15 min)

  • Share a story or video (e.g., local Elder’s teaching or a book like "The Four Sacred Elements”).
  • Encourage students to listen for connections between the elements and themselves or the land.

3: Guided Group Discussion (10 min)
  • Create a class chart for each element.
  • Ask: What does fire feel like? What does water teach us? How do we experience air? What grows in the earth?
  • Record student responses under each element.

4: Hands-On Activity (15–20 min)
  • Distribute blank Medicine Wheel templates.
  • Guide students to label each quadrant with the correct element, direction, and color:
    • Air = north
    • Earth = west
    • Fire = south
    • Water = west
  • Students draw or write about what each element means to them personally (e.g., “Water reminds me of swimming in the lake with my family.”)

5: Optional Outdoor Connection (if time & weather permit)
  • Go on a short nature walk.
  • Ask students to notice where they feel the elements around them (wind on their face, sun on skin, trees in soil, puddles or clouds).

6: Reflection & Sharing Circle (10 min)
  • Return to the circle and invite students to share one element they feel most connected to and why.
  • Emphasize listening, respect, and gratitude.

Assessment & Extension

Assessment:

  • Observe student participation and engagement in circle discussions.
  • Completed Medicine Wheel element worksheet (accuracy and personal reflection).
  • Observation of thoughtful sharing and respectful listening.

Extensions:

  • Create class mural with four quadrants representing each element and student contributions.
  • Explore how different cultures use the elements in ceremonies or stories.
  • Invite a local Elder or Knowledge Keeper to speak about the significance of the elements in their teachings.

Stages of Life on the Medicine Wheel

Grade Level: 5
Duration: 60 minutes
Subjects:Social Studies, Health & Career Education, Art
Location: Classroom & (optional) Outdoors

Big Ideas (BC Curriculum):

  • Understanding the past helps us make sense of the present.
  • Our beliefs, values, and connections to others help shape who we are.
  • Indigenous knowledge is passed down through oral history, stories, and traditions.

First Peoples Principles of Learning:

  • Learning is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential, and relational.
  • Learning involves recognizing the consequences of one’s actions.
  • Learning is embedded in memory, history, and story.
  • Learning involves exploration of one’s identity.

Learning Objectives:

Students will:

  • Identify the four stages of life represented in the Medicine Wheel.
  • Understand the meanings and responsibilities associated with each life stage.
  • Review the directional, color, and seasonal associations of each stage.
  • Reflect on their own life stage and explore how teachings relate to personal growth.
  • Respectfully engage with Indigenous knowledge through story, discussion, and creative work.

Materials:

  • Large visual of the Medicine Wheel showing stages of life
  • Printable Medicine Wheel templates (students will add the stages of life)
  • Blank Medicine Wheel Template
  • Colored pencils/crayons (white, yellow, red, black)
  • Age-appropriate story, video, or Elder recording about life stages
  • Reflection journal or writing paper
  • Chart paper and markers
  • Small talking stick (or other object) or sharing item (optional for circle time)

Lesson Activities:

1: Introduction & Circle Discussion (10–15 min)

Sit in a circle:

  • Begin with a land acknowledgment.
  • Ask: “What do you know about the stages of life? What do we learn at different times in our life?”
  • Introduce the Medicine Wheel as a guide for living and growth.
  • Briefly explain the four stages of life:
    • Elder (north) Wisdom, teaching, peace
    • Infant (east) New beginnings, curiosity, openness
    • Youth (south) Leaning, identity, energy
    • Adult (west) Responsibility, reflection, care for others


2: Story or Video (10–15 min)

  • Share a story from a book or Elder teaching about growing through life stages.
  • Encourage students to listen for teachings from each stage and how they relate to the wheel
  • Example book: “The Four Seasons of Life” (if available) or a local Indigenous voice via video.

3: Guided Group Discussion (10 min)
  • Create a class anchor chart divided into four quadrants: Infancy, Youth, Adulthood, Elder.
  • Ask: “What do we learn as a baby? What are some things youth need to know? What does adulthood bring? Why are Elders important?”
  • Write student ideas in each section.Record student responses under each element.

4: Hands-On Activity (15–20 min)
  • Hand out the blank Medicine Wheel life stages templates.
  • Students color each quadrant and label:
    • Elder (north/white)
    • Infant (east/yellow)
    • Youth (south/red)
    • Adult (west/black)
  • Example: “As a youth, I’m learning who I am.”

5: Optional Outdoor Connection (if time & weather permit)
  • Go outside and reflect quietly: “What do you see or feel that reminds you of your stage in life?”
  • Encourage drawing or journaling about the experience back inside.

6: Sharing Circle & Reflection (10 min)
  • Return to the circle and invite students to share one thing they wrote or drew.
  • Prompt: “What stage are you in now? What do you think is important about it?”
  • Emphasize respect, listening, and gratitude.

Assessment & Extension

Assessment:

  • Observe student participation and engagement in circle discussions.
  • Review completed Medicine Wheel templates for understanding and personal insight.
  • Anecdotal notes on respectful listening and sharing.

Extensions:

What is the Medicine Wheel? (video)

When introducing the Medicine Wheel to students, it is important for educators to recognize that there is no single, universal way to teach or understand this sacred symbol. Teachings about the Medicine Wheel vary across Indigenous Nations, each reflecting the unique worldview, language, and traditions of that community. While the colors, directions, and associated meanings may differ, the Medicine Wheel commonly represents interconnectedness, balance, and holistic well-being. By acknowledging the diversity of teachings, educators honor the rich cultural knowledge of Indigenous Peoples and foster a respectful and inclusive learning environment. When possible, connecting with local Elders or Knowledge Keepers can help ensure that teachings are shared in a culturally appropriate and meaningful way.



Body & Mind Health on the Medicine Wheel

Grade Level: 6
Duration: 1–2 class periods (60–90 mins total)
Subjects: Art Education, Social Studies, Health & Physical Education
Location: Classroom & Outdoors

Big Ideas (BC Curriculum):

  • Learning about Indigenous perspectives helps us understand the importance of balance and respect in our lives and communities.
  • Systems and ideas from Indigenous cultures offer holistic approaches to health, identity, and interconnectedness.

First Peoples Principles of Learning:

  • Learning is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential, and relational.
  • Learning involves generational roles and responsibilities.
  • Learning recognizes Indigenous knowledge systems and worldviews.

Learning Objectives:

Students will:

  • Understand the four quadrants of the Medicine Wheel (mental, physical, emotional, spiritual).
  • Explore how the Medicine Wheel is used to support well-being and balance.
  • Reflect on how these teachings relate to their own lives.
  • Respectfully engage with Indigenous worldviews and teachings.

Materials:

  • Visual of the Medicine Wheel with labeled directions, colors, seasons, and meanings
  • Blank Medicine Wheel Template
  • Chart paper or whiteboard
  • Markers or colored pencils
  • Reflection journal or writing paper
  • Small talking stick (or other object) or sharing item (optional for circle time)

Lesson Activities:

1: Opening Circle / Introduction (10–15 min)

Sit in a circle:

  • Begin with a respectful land acknowledgment (challenge students to come up with a meaningful, unconventional, land acknowledgment).
  • Ask students: “What does being healthy mean to you?” Write student ideas on the board.
  • Introduce the Medicine Wheel as a holistic way many Indigenous cultures view health and balance.
  • Depending on students understanding, or what they remember, you may need to remind them that the Medicine Wheel is used by many Indigenous communities as a guide for balance and well-being (and there are many interpretations across the nations).

2: Explore the Medicine Wheel (15–20 min)

  • Show a visual of the Medicine Wheel (with white on top, yellow on right, red on bottom, black on left).
  • Remind students that about the different aspects of the Medicine Wheel they may have learned about in the past, (eg., white is in the direction of the north and the season is winter; yellow is in the direction of the east and the season is spring; red is in the direction of the south and the season is summer; black is in the direction of the west and the season is fall).

  • Discuss the meaning of each quadrant:
    • Spiritual (north) - beliefs, connection to something greater
    • Mental (east) - thinking, learning
    • Physical (south) – body, activity
    • Emotional (west) feelings, relationships
  • Emphasize that meanings and interpretations can vary by Nation and that this is just one version.


3: Student Reflection Activity (20–30 min)

  • Brainstorm and record students ideas on how they take care of their own body, mind, emotions
    and spiritual needs. (Remember that spiritual is not necessarily religion.)
  • Provide students with a blank Medicine Wheel template.
  • Ask them to draw or write examples of how they take care of their body, mind, emotions, and spirit.
  • Share in partners or small groups (optional sharing with class).

4: Wrap-Up Discussion (10 min)
  • What did you learn from the Medicine Wheel today?
  • How can this way of thinking help us stay balanced and healthy?
  • Option: Have students write a short paragraph about what they discovered about balance in their life
    in relation to the Medicine Wheel.

Assessment & Extension

Assessment:

  • Observation during discussion and sharing
  • Completion and thoughtfulness in Medicine Wheel reflection
  • Optional: Exit slip → “One thing I learned from the Medicine Wheel is...”

Extensions:

  • Invite an Indigenous Elder or Knowledge Keeper to speak (with appropriate protocols).
  • Create a classroom “Wellness Wheel” mural.
  • Connect with Science or Health units on systems of the body, mental wellness, or emotional regulation.

Sacred Medicines on the Medicine Wheel

Grade Level: 7
Duration: 1–2 class periods (60–90 mins total)
Subjects: Art Education, Social Studies, Health & Physical Education
Location: Classroom & Outdoors

Big Ideas (BC Curriculum):

  • Learning about Indigenous perspectives helps us understand the importance of balance and respect in our lives and communities.
  • Indigenous knowledge systems provide holistic and meaningful approaches to health, culture, and well-being.

First Peoples Principles of Learning:

  • Learning is holistic, reflexive, reflective, experiential, and relational.
  • Learning involves generational roles and responsibilities.
  • Learning recognizes Indigenous knowledge systems and worldviews.

Learning Objectives:

Students will:

  • Identify the four sacred medicines: sage, sweetgrass, cedar, and tobacco.
  • Understand the traditional roles and teachings of each medicine.
  • Explore the connections between the sacred medicines and the four quadrants of the Medicine Wheel.
  • Reflect on how Indigenous teachings about wellness relate to their own health and identity.

Materials:

  • Visual of the Medicine Wheel with labeled directions, seasons, animals, and stages of life
  • Printable visuals or real samples of sage, sweetgrass, cedar, and tobacco (if available)
  • Blank Medicine Wheel Template
  • Chart paper or whiteboard
  • Markers or colored pencils
  • Reflection journal or writing paper
  • Cultural Protocols Poster

  • Small talking stick (or other object) or sharing item (optional for circle time)

Lesson Activities:

1: Opening Circle / Introduction (10–15 min)

Sit in a circle:

  • Begin with a respectful land acknowledgment (challenge students to come up with a meaningful, unconventional, land acknowledgment).
  • Ask students: “What do you already know about sacred medicines?”.
  • Introduce the Medicine Wheel as a framework used by many Indigenous Nations for balance, healing, and understanding the world, (and there are many interpretations across the nations).
  • Briefly review the quadrants: Spiritual (white/north), Mental (yellow/east), Physical (red/south), Emotional (black/west).

2: Teachings of the Sacred Medicines (20–30 min)

  • Hand out the blank Medicine Wheel life stages templates.
  • Introduce the four sacred medicines:
    • Sage (often linked with cleansing and wisdom)
    • Sweetgrass (used in ceremony, symbolizes kindness and connection)
    • Cedar (used in protection, healing, and purification)
    • Tobacco (used in offerings and prayer, for respect and communication)
  • Explain that each medicine has unique teachings and can be connected to one or more quadrants of the Medicine Wheel.
  • Highlight that these medicines are used respectfully in ceremony and that traditions may differ by community.
  • Show real samples if appropriate and permitted.


    • 3: Personal Reflection Activity (20–30 min)

    • On chart paper, brainstorm how each medicine might support one’s well-being in the physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual aspects.
    • Distribute the blank Medicine Wheel template.
    • Students draw or write one idea in each quadrant connecting it to one of the sacred medicines (e.g., “Sweetgrass helps my emotions because its smell makes me feel calm.”)
    • Invite optional sharing in pairs, small groups, or a talking circle.

    4: Closing Discussion (10 min)
    • Ask students: What medicine or quadrant did you feel most connected to today?
    • How do you think using these teachings could help a community?
    • Option: Write a short reflection paragraph.

    Assessment & Extension

    Assessment:

    • Active participation in discussion and circle time
    • Thoughtful completion of Medicine Wheel reflection worksheet
    • Optional exit slip: "One thing I learned about sacred medicines is..."

    Extensions:

    • Invite an Elder or Knowledge Keeper to share teachings (with protocols)
    • Use art to create a collective Medicine Wheel poster with visuals of the medicines
      • Explore how the medicines relate to emotional regulation, mental focus, physical practices, and spiritual reflection
